Scope: a trusted delivery and financing arrangement

All parties

Every Home Metered offers utilities and mini-grid operators a verified metering service. One accountable Metering Programme Operator, within an existing organisation, groups procurement, installation and maintenance under standard agreements, and an existing licensed bank executes the agreed payment rules. There is no new bank and no new public institution.

Households and small businesses are the beneficiaries. Their protections remain in scope; detailed B2C design follows as a separate exercise.

Decision sought

Government

Approval to prepare a bounded, costed pilot within an existing host organisation. A capped household metering charge and an explicit utility service payment are proposed, with capital grants and operating support where required.

No funds, bank, partner or tariff change is assumed to be committed. The design promise is no upfront meter payment; cheaper electricity, uninterrupted supply and self-financing meters are not claimed.

Response to the brief

All parties

Proposed approach: a cost-sharing framework, delivered as meter-as-a-service, with results-based utility payments.

Households pay a small capped metering charge with each energy purchase; the utility pays an explicit contribution tied to verified gains; government provides bounded first-loss funding from money already earmarked. Meters are supplied, installed and maintained as a service, and providers are paid per functioning meter.

Starter questionResponse
1. Who should pay for a meter, and why is that fair?Those who benefit, in proportion: households a small capped charge (they get a verifiable bill and maintained service); the utility a contribution from verified cash gains and loss reduction (it gains most); government bounded first loss (a public good, using earmarked funds). Nobody pays twice: the charge replaces the existing metering surcharge and earlier buyers are credited.
2. How can a meter be paid for without a large upfront charge?Capital grants and, later, affordable debt pay for meters upfront through a protected account at an existing licensed bank. Households repay part as a percentage of each token; providers are paid only on verified commissioning.
3. Where should the first million meters go?Meters already delivered but not installed, then ready feeders in low-metering, high-loss areas, with a fairness floor for every region. Within each feeder: low-income unmetered households, people owed refunds, and faulty or obsolete meters first. See Geographic rollout.
4. How do you protect the model from an import duty or currency drop?Contract clauses: price split and indexed with a supplier risk band, batch prices fixed at call-off, change-in-law clause placing duty changes with the funding authority, local kit assembly, milestone payments and consignment stock. The household charge stays capped. See Contracts & payments.
5. How do you keep free-meter and paid-meter customers from feeling treated unfairly?One rule for everyone from day one: the same capped metering charge for upkeep, whoever paid for the meter. Earlier buyers’ outstanding refunds become automatic credits on every token. New meters are allocated by need and readiness, not tariff band.

Note on gaps: the model deliberately covers investment in years 1–5 only. Shaded years show income and maintenance for meters already installed, but exclude spending that will be required: meters for new customers (about 0.5M a year), continuing programme running costs, and from year 11 the replacement of meters reaching the end of their 10-year life (roughly – a year). Figures in shaded rows therefore understate the funding need and must not be read as a complete projection.

Note: the 15-year horizon (five years of rollout plus a 10-year meter life) shows that household charges and utility payments do not repay the meters within their life. On a payback basis the business case is therefore weak: meter capital is a public investment in a functioning, maintained service, and the case rests on utility, fiscal and household benefits rather than on cost recovery. The horizon also excludes spending after year 5 on meters for new customers, the replacement of meters reaching the end of their life from year 11, and continuing programme running costs; these are to be added before the case is presented to funders.

Calculation review: corrections and remaining limitations

All parties

Corrected in this version, following the cross-check with the ChatGPT and Cursor review

  • Benefit–cost ratio now excludes the unverified feeder-loss recovery; the ratio including it is shown separately.
  • The lifetime “who pays” chart no longer counts loan proceeds and receipts twice; interest on conditional debt is shown as a cost.
  • The maintenance gap is taken from the cash flow and includes the period before the first receipts.
  • Solar and mini-grid connections are costed at the standard meter-cost proxy and funded by rural electrification grants, instead of being counted without cost.
  • Supplier payment milestones total 100% (15%, 45%, 30%, 10%).
  • Labels distinguish conditional debt capacity and required public capital from confirmed funding; earmarked Nigerian programme funds are reference only.
  • An additional backbone budget input is available (zero until priced).

Remaining limitations

  • Spending after year 5 (new customers, end-of-life replacement, continuing running costs) and B2C costs are excluded.
  • Installations are not constrained by funding availability; the geographic fairness floor is not enforced in the national calculation.
  • Failures in the existing (pre-programme) fleet are represented only through the legacy-replacement share.
  • The utility’s incremental supply and collection costs are not deducted from its cash gain; inactive-meter months approximate energy purchases.
  • Debt capacity applies a nominal naira rate to receipts held at today’s level, which is conservative; real and nominal treatment is to be confirmed with lenders.
  • A 10-year meter life is assumed; the NERC 120-month refund period is a reference, not evidence of physical life.
  • Contract terms, supplier risk bands and repair limits are proposals requiring counterparties and regulatory approval.

Problem and design challenge

All parties

Provide households with functioning, maintained meters through one trusted service interface, without upfront meter payments, using transparent charges, an explicit utility contribution and clearly bounded public support.

Problem. Millions of households lack functioning electricity meters and face bills they cannot verify. Upfront costs, fragmented delivery, unclear maintenance responsibilities and conflicting incentives make getting and keeping a meter difficult. This erodes trust, weakens payments and limits funding for further rollout.

Design challenge. How might we give every household access to functioning, maintained metering within five years without large upfront payments or unaffordable ongoing charges, while keeping utilities financially viable and public funding requirements transparent?

This programme is for metering and accountable service. It does not promise improvements such as cheaper electricity, uninterrupted supply or installation cost recovery that the evidence does not support.

The argument for the set-up

Metering Programme Operator

Metering Programme Operator: a clearly mandated delivery function within an existing organisation, not a new institution. Government retains funding authority; an existing licensed bank executes agreed payment rules, with independent reconciliation. Statutory powers, procurement accountability and independent regulation are preserved.

Delivery gap it fillsDuplicated task it removes
No one resolves delivery failures across contractorsEach DisCo, programme and provider chasing its own handoffs
No proof that meters work after installationSeparate installation reporting to NERC, fund managers and DisCos
No single view of expenditure, complaints and maintenanceParallel monthly returns in different formats
Late warning of procurement delays and funding gapsProgramme-by-programme procurement tracking
No way to replace a failing provider without abandoning customersCustomer-by-customer escalation to the regulator

What it offers government

  • One party responsible for resolving delivery failures across contractors.
  • Verified functioning meters, rather than installation announcements alone.
  • Clear visibility of expenditure, customer complaints and maintenance.
  • Earlier warning of procurement delays and funding shortfalls.
  • A way to stop or replace underperforming providers without abandoning customers.

Conditions

  • Full cost disclosed, with the work and costs it replaces; net benefit demonstrated. If an existing organisation can deliver the same through a clearer mandate, a separate operator is unnecessary.
  • Affordability, existing entitlements and prevention of duplicate charging are explicit pilot conditions.
  • Delegated operational decisions defined precisely.
  • Bounded pilot, capped expenditure, termination rights and an orderly handover plan.

The strongest argument: the arrangement helps government, regulators and utilities deliver their existing commitments with fewer unresolved handoffs and clearer evidence of results.

Protection against change

  • Duty or levy change after award: change-in-law clause; cost to the funding authority, not households or suppliers.
  • Regulatory change (standards, token rules): costed variation, agreed through the change board.
  • Currency shock: risk band above; forward cover for confirmed batches where affordable.
  • Port or shipping delay: staged shipments; delay damages; title and risk pass at acceptance.
  • Supplier failure: performance bond; dual sourcing; step-in and transfer to the second supplier.
  • Technology change: upgrade path to smart meters at agreed prices; no proprietary lock-in.

Every input is listed with its value (blue box), unit, how solid it is (Researched, Proxy, Design choice, Guess), its influence on the result and a source note (yellow box).
